## The question

Computing education borrows its learning theories from elsewhere — from psychology, from
maths education, from the learning sciences generally. Borrowed well, they explain a great
deal. Borrowed loosely, they turn into vocabulary.

So: which theories does the field actually use, how is it using them, and can that use be
organised into something a researcher or a teacher can navigate?

## What I made

A contribution to an ITiCSE 2019 working group, which met in Aberdeen in July and reported
as _Review and use of learning theories within computer science education research: primer
for researchers and practitioners_ — nine authors, twenty-one pages, in the working group
proceedings.

The report is a quantitative analysis of how the computing education research communities
adapt the theories they borrow, written to help researchers see what the discipline is
currently doing with them. ==The word in the subtitle that matters is "primer": the output
is a working document for people who need to pick a theory and use it properly, rather
than an argument aimed at other theorists.==

The working group also produced a short companion paper, _A Periodic Table of Computing
Education Learning Theories_, which is the two-page version of the same idea and has ten
authors rather than nine.

## What I did myself

One contributor of nine, and not the lead. ==What I took from it was less the byline than
the room: a week of watching researchers argue about what a theory is for, which is a
different and more useful thing than reading the results afterwards.==

I was the only author not at a university. ==That is the part worth noting — the
collaboration put someone whose job is making classroom resources alongside people who
study whether such resources work, which is the relationship the rest of this site keeps
circling.==
