## The question

Computing education borrows its learning theories from elsewhere, from psychology, from maths education, from the learning sciences generally. Borrowed well, they explain a great deal. Borrowed loosely, they turn into vocabulary.

So: which theories does the field actually use, how is it using them, and can that use be organised into something a researcher or a teacher can navigate?

## What I made

A contribution to an ITiCSE 2019 working group, which met in Aberdeen in July and reported as _Review and use of learning theories within computer science education research: primer for researchers and practitioners_ nine authors, twenty-one pages, in the working group proceedings.

The report is a quantitative analysis of how the computing education research communities adapt the theories they borrow, written to help researchers see what the discipline is currently doing with them. The word in the subtitle that matters is "primer": the output is a working document for people who need to pick a theory and use it properly, rather than an argument aimed at other theorists.

The map at the top of this page is the part I find most useful. Sixty-nine theories, 1,136 links between them, clustered by modularity into three groups the report names **experiential theories**, **theories of mind** and **social theories**.

![The orange community on its own: Constructivism at the top, linked out to Situated Cognition, Cognitive Apprenticeship, Social Development Theory, Constructionism, the Zone of Proximal Development, Scaffolding, Communities of Practice and Situated Learning, with heavier lines showing the strongest links.](assets/img/learning-theories-social-community.png)

The social cluster is the one I align most closely with, Constructionism sits in it, a few links from Scaffolding and the Zone of Proximal Development, which is roughly where I would put myself too. There is more on that in [[research-and-pedagogy|where I stand]].
